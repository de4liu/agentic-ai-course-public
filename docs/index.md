## About the course

Generative AI has moved from novelty to operational infrastructure in under
three years, and agentic AI, systems that plan, act, and coordinate
autonomously, is accelerating that shift. Most business graduates enter the
workforce able to use a chatbot, but without the conceptual vocabulary to
evaluate AI claims, the workflow intuition to spot automation opportunities,
or the grounding to lead AI initiatives. This course addresses that gap.

The course is designed for business majors who will manage AI tools,
commission AI projects, and make adoption decisions, rather than build AI
from scratch. The goal is informed fluency: what AI can and cannot do, how
to translate business problems into AI solutions, and how to lead
responsibly in AI-augmented organizations. It is open to all Carlson
concentrations and requires no prior technical background.

## Course design

Each week has two 100-minute sessions. The first introduces concepts through
short lectures, live demonstrations and case discussion; the second is
hands-on, with students building AI workflows and agent designs using
no-code tools (Gemini, Google AI Studio, Make, and n8n running locally in
Docker).

Three **build sprints** anchor the applied work: students ship a workflow
automation, build a first agent, then red-team a peer's agent and harden
their own. Assessment also includes four reflection posts, a midterm and
final (each split into a closed-book part and an open-AI part), weekly
check-ins, and a team capstone worth 25% of the grade, presented at the AI
Innovation Showcase.

## Learning by doing, every session

Every session includes at least one structured activity. Students audit,
argue, build, break and pitch; lecture segments stay short.

<div class="objective-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="objective-card" markdown="1">
<h3>Structured in-class activities</h3>
<p>Personal task audits, agent blueprint cards, case analysis (a help-desk agent that closed everything), vendor-question drills, pitch rehearsals.</p>
</div>
<div class="objective-card" markdown="1">
<h3>Debates with a position</h3>
<p>Does AI multiply a team or replace it? Students take a side, defend it, and confront the strongest counterargument.</p>
</div>
<div class="objective-card" markdown="1">
<h3>Live demos, real failure</h3>
<p>Models vary, hallucinate and break on screen. Students learn verification by watching it go wrong, not by being told.</p>
</div>
<div class="objective-card" markdown="1">
<h3>Three build sprints</h3>
<p>A workflow automation, a first agent, then a red-team round: attack a peer's build, harden your own, document what you found.</p>
</div>
<div class="objective-card" markdown="1">
<h3>Authentic assessment</h3>
<p>The capstone is pitched live to external guests at the AI Innovation Showcase and scored like a real pitch, not a class presentation.</p>
</div>
<div class="objective-card" markdown="1">
<h3>Reflection as a habit</h3>
<p>Four graded posts on what you did, how you checked it, and what you would change, building verification into the routine.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 id="the-arc">Weekly outline</h2>

| Week | Theme | Session 1 (concepts) | Session 2 (hands-on) |
|:----:|-------|----------------------|----------------------|
| 1 | GenAI Foundations | How AI Works and How to Work With It | Beyond Chat: Workspaces, Connectors, Agents, and Enterprise RAG |
| 2 | Workflow Automation | Workflow Thinking: What Gets Automated | Build Sprint 1: Your First Automation |
| 3 | Agents and Capstone Kickoff | What Is an AI Agent? | Capstone Launch and Team Formation |
| 4 | Guardrails and Agents | Failure Modes, Guardrails, and Checking at Scale | Agents in the Wild and Build Start |
| 5 | Build and Responsible AI | Midterm Part A, then Build Sprint 2: Your First Agent | Responsible AI |
| 6 | Red Team and Enterprise AI | Build Sprint 3: Red Team and Fix | AI in the Enterprise and Idea Pitching |
| 7 | Showcase and Strategy | AI Innovation Showcase | Strategy and the Long View |

<h2>AI Innovation Showcase</h2>

<p class="showcase-lede" markdown="1">
The course ends with a trade-show-style showcase rather than a final
presentation day. Cross-concentration student teams staff stations while
practitioners, faculty and guests rotate through, spending several minutes
with each team: the problem, the design, the business value, what could go
wrong, and what comes next.
</p>

<div class="showcase-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="showcase-item" markdown="1">
<h3>External audience</h3>
<p>Guests include practitioners who make real AI decisions. Questions are unscripted, and honest uncertainty scores better than bluffing.</p>
</div>
<div class="showcase-item" markdown="1">
<h3>Judged like a pitch</h3>
<p>Guests and peers score each team on problem and business value, solution design, responsible AI, and the quality of pitch and answers.</p>
</div>
<div class="showcase-item" markdown="1">
<h3>Show the experience</h3>
<p>Every team walks guests through the user experience with a mock, screens or a storyboard. A working no-code demo is a bonus, not a requirement.</p>
</div>
